Filings · § 1514

Inside the 180-day protest window.

Why "180 days" is not one deadline but many, what liquidation actually means, and how the calendar runs differently for every entry on your books.

Refund Harbor · Commentary

May 27, 2026 · ~6 min read

The line you keep hearing is “180 days.” It’s correct, but it can suggest a single calendar deadline — a date on a wall where, if you miss it, the whole opportunity is gone. That isn’t quite how the statute works, and the difference matters for any importer with more than a handful of 2025 entries.

What 19 U.S.C. § 1514 actually says

The customs protest statute, 19 U.S.C. § 1514, gives an importer the right to file a written protest with U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) challenging certain CBP decisions — including the appraisement and assessment of duties on an imported entry. The deadline to file is generally 180 days from the date of liquidation of the entry. After that window closes for a given entry, CBP’s decision on that entry is treated as final.

The clock you have to watch is liquidation, not entry

Importers think in terms of entry — the date a shipment lands and an entry summary is filed. The protest deadline doesn’t run from entry. It runs from liquidation, which is the formal final calculation of duties on that entry by CBP. Liquidation can take anywhere from a few months to over a year after entry, and it is not always announced with fanfare. The official notice typically posts on the importer’s ACE portal account.

Two practical consequences:

  • An entry from early 2025 that has already liquidated may have weeks, not months, of protest window left.
  • An entry from late 2025 that hasn’t liquidated yet may have more time than you think — the 180 days hasn’t started.

Every entry on the books has its own clock. For an importer with hundreds or thousands of entries across 2025, there are hundreds or thousands of clocks, and they aren’t synchronized.

Why this is the work, not the marketing

Most of the difficulty in a tariff refund engagement isn’t writing a single protest. It’s the inventory step: pulling each entry summary (CBP Form 7501), identifying the liquidation date for each one, and ordering the universe of entries by the day each protest window closes. Without that map, even a well-drafted protest can miss entries that quietly liquidated months earlier.

The map also tells you the order of operations. Entries with the nearest deadline get protested first. Entries not yet liquidated get a calendar reminder, an ACE-portal watch, and a protest queued for filing once the clock starts. Entries already past 180 days from liquidation are checked for any preserved remedy — sometimes a parallel court filing has held the door open — but generally treated as final.

What counts as “filed”

A protest under § 1514 isn’t a letter. It’s a formal submission with specific content requirements: identification of the entries, statement of the decision being challenged, the basis for the protest, and the relief sought. CBP’s system of record is ACE. A protest is properly filed when it’s submitted in the form and manner CBP accepts, which in practice means an attorney or licensed customs broker working in ACE with a valid Customs Power of Attorney for the importer of record.

The deadline isn’t the only deadline

Once a protest is filed, CBP has its own clock to act on it. A denied protest opens a further window to seek judicial review at the U.S. Court of International Trade (CIT) — a specialized federal court. Those CIT deadlines are different from the § 1514 window and require their own discipline. For most importers, those later deadlines are not the concern in May 2026; for any importer whose protest is denied later this year, they will be.

What to do with all of this

Three concrete things.

  • Pull your 2025 entries now. Even just a list with entry dates, ports, and goods is enough for an initial pass. Liquidation dates are the next layer, and they live in ACE.
  • Treat “180 days” as a sliding window per entry, not one calendar date. The entries with the oldest liquidations are the most time-sensitive, regardless of when they entered.
  • Don’t wait for a portal notice to act. By the time CBP’s notice has fully propagated, you may have already burned weeks.
