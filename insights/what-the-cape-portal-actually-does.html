What CBP's CAPE portal actually does.

A look under the hood at Phase 1 processing, the documentation it really wants, and why roughly 40% of early submissions have been kicked back.

Refund Harbor · Commentary

May 27, 2026 · ~7 min read

CBP stood up the CAPE portal as a faster track for IEEPA refund requests after the February 2026 ruling. The marketing line is “60 to 90 days.” The reality is that CAPE works well for entries that fit a specific profile — and rejects, often for procedural reasons, the ones that don’t. Knowing the difference saves importers months.

Two phases, not one

CAPE isn’t a single conveyor belt. It runs two phases:

  • Phase 1 handles entries that are unliquidated, or recently liquidated, with clean and complete documentation. These are the “60 to 90 days” cases.
  • Phase 2 handles historically liquidated entries — the longer tail of older paid-in entries. Phase 2 timelines run 12 to 18 months, sometimes longer.

If your 2025 entries are split between recent and older, your case is also split: part in Phase 1, part in Phase 2. That split is normal. What is not normal — and what causes most early rejections — is sending Phase 2-style entries through Phase 1 on the assumption that “the portal will sort it out.” The portal does not sort it out. It rejects them.

What the portal actually wants

CAPE’s submission requirements are, by federal-portal standards, conservative. Each entry needs:

  • The entry summary (CBP Form 7501), intact and legible.
  • The commercial invoice matching the entry.
  • The HTS classification that was actually used at entry, with the duty rate applied.
  • Evidence of the IEEPA tariff component within the duty paid — i.e., the portion now eligible for refund.
  • A valid ACE-portal account tied to the importer of record, with submission authority demonstrated.

Each one looks simple on a single entry. Across hundreds of entries, the work is in the reconciliation: ensuring the invoice, 7501, and duty payment all line up to the same cents on the same entry number, with the IEEPA component isolated. Spreadsheets help. Spreadsheets are not enough.

Why ~40% of early filings have been rejected

Early rejection rates on CAPE Phase 1 are running near 40%. From what the rejection notices say, the causes cluster into four buckets:

  • Wrong phase. Older liquidated entries pushed through Phase 1 submissions get bounced for review under Phase 2 instead.
  • Documentation mismatch. The invoice and the 7501 don’t agree on units, value, or HTS line items. Often a clerical drift; always a rejection.
  • Authority gaps. The submitting party doesn’t have a current Customs Power of Attorney on file with CBP for the importer of record. Brokers and attorneys both have to have this in place; expired ones are surprisingly common.
  • Wrong duty component flagged. Submissions that ask for refund of duties unrelated to IEEPA (or that mix IEEPA tariffs with section 232/301 amounts without isolating them) get kicked.

None of these are merits questions. CBP is not deciding whether you were entitled to a refund. It’s deciding whether your submission is well-formed. A rejection at this stage rarely kills the underlying claim, but it costs weeks — and weeks against a 180-day protest window that runs from liquidation are not free.

What a clean Phase 1 submission looks like

Three discipline points:

  • Phase before everything else. Each entry is sorted into Phase 1 or Phase 2 by its liquidation status before any document gets attached.
  • One source of truth per entry. 7501, invoice, and HTS classification are reconciled into one canonical line per entry. If they disagree, the disagreement is resolved before submission, not after.
  • Authority in place first. The Customs Power of Attorney is current, on file with CBP, and identifies the submitting party. ACE account access is verified before the first filing.

What Phase 2 actually looks like

For historically liquidated entries, Phase 2 isn’t a slower CAPE — it’s a different posture. Phase 2 entries typically need to be challenged through formal protest under § 1514, which means the calendar from liquidation, not portal submission, is what controls. Importers heavy in Phase 2 entries are well served by running protest preparation in parallel with any Phase 1 work, because the windows don’t wait.

The shorter version

CAPE is a fast lane for clean entries. It is not a forgiving system, and its rejection rate is not random — it tracks documentation discipline more than anything else. Importers who treat the portal as a clearing system get burned; importers who treat it as a federal filing with strict acceptance rules tend to come out the other side in 60 to 90 days.
